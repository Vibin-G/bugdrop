Frame — Design Findings

What the research turned up on your time-aware photography screensaver, and how it changes the build.

The finding that reshapes your plan

The app you described — a random pull of starred photos across your whole library, captioned with location — cannot be built on Google's live APIs. This is not a difficulty to engineer around. It's a wall, and it's better to know now than in week three.

  • The photoslibrary.readonly scope was removed on 31 March 2025. The favorites query you're imagining now returns 403 PERMISSION_DENIED. The Library API only sees photos your own app uploaded.
  • The Picker API, Google's replacement, expires each selection after seven days and its image links after about sixty minutes. It also strips GPS by design. An unattended frame would blank weekly and could never show a place name.
  • There is an API built for exactly this — the Ambient API, for slideshows on TVs and photo frames. Access requires acceptance into Google's partner program. Apply in week one, because approval is slow; let nothing depend on it.

The way through: Google Takeout's per-photo sidecar files carry favorited, geoData latitude and longitude, and photoTakenTime — precisely the three fields every live API now withholds. That makes Takeout your primary source, not a fallback, which is the opposite of how nearly everyone starts this build.

The architectural consequence

One rule follows directly, and it isn't negotiable: never stream from Google at display time. Copy the bytes into your own storage at import, extract metadata once, and serve the display entirely from your own infrastructure.

The seven-day and sixty-minute expiries force it. The happy side effect: once you own the copies, the next policy change can't break your frame. Cloudflare fits well and matches muscle memory you already have — Workers for the API, R2 for images (no bandwidth charges, which matters when a frame pulls a photo every thirty seconds all day), D1 for metadata, and Queues so a forty-gigabyte import never blocks a request.

Two ideas worth stealing

Neither of these is obvious, and both are high leverage. The first is pairing codes. The devices best suited to being a photo frame — an old iPad, a cheap tablet, a TV browser — are exactly the worst for typing an email and password. So don't make them: the display shows a six-character code, and you bind it from your phone.

The second is night mode instead of off. When the scheduled hours end, don't blank the screen — fade the photograph and hold a dimmed clock. That one decision turns an idle tablet into a bedside clock, which is what actually keeps the device plugged in between trips.

Sharing it with other people

A full Takeout export is far too heavy an ask for a guest, so tier the ingest paths by user. You import Takeout once and get the whole starred archive with locations intact. Guests get drag-and-drop upload — where EXIF location survives untouched — plus the Picker. Everyone lands in the same store.

One caution matters more than the rest: a shared link exposing coordinates of photos shot at someone's home is a real harm, not a theoretical one. Resolve coordinates to a place name at import, keep the precise values private, and never return raw coordinates from the API.

What was delivered

The full brief is published as a web page, opening with a working prototype of the display. The clock in it is real and running. Two toggles show the behaviour: one simulates 11:40 PM so you can watch night mode take over, the other switches on WanderCalm trip captions, so a photograph reads Day 4 of Italy 2026 rather than just Amalfi Coast.

The brief and the single-file prototype are committed to the claude/photography-screensaver-design-5k778c branch under docs/. Worth noting they landed in the bugdrop repo, since that's the only repo this session could reach — say the word if they belong in a WanderCalm repo instead.

On verification, so you know what was checked rather than assumed: the prototype was rendered in a real browser in both themes — no console errors, no sideways scrolling, and the clock, subscripted date and both branches of the caption fallback confirmed working on screen. The imagery is procedurally generated stand-in artwork, not photographs.
